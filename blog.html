Personality Expression and Perception in LLMs & Humans

November 03, 2026

My first paper got published: you can access it here Let me provide you with a little background. What started out as a thesis almost 2 years ago has since grown into a fully fledged research endeavour; but early concepts of robotics and artificial intelligence can be traced back as far as human imagination itself. Throughout history, Artificial Intelligence (AI) always possessed a mysterious allure. The intriguing borderline between human and machine has long been a subject of fascination for science fiction writers, cognitive scientists and engineers alike. Across various art forms, robots, their creation, their human-like characteristics and differences and its relation to humans have been explored, revealing a spark of mysticism and a tentative attraction to the uncanny. In the 18th canto of Iliad, Homer surrounded his paralyzed character Hephaestus with autonomous android women who assisted him (Homer, 800 B.C). Centuries later, E.T.A. Hoffmann's "Der Sandmann" (originally published 1816) depicts the narrator's infatuation with a lifelike automaton named "Olimpia". The story was reinvented in the famous ballet “Coppelia” (Saint-Léon & Delibes, 1870), as well as depicted in a movie by Georges Méliès (1900) - with both adaptations featuring the robotic, inanimate puppet Olimpia as its namesake. In 1927, the silent film "Metropolis" by Fritz Lang took the idea further, turning a human woman, Maria, into the mechanical duplicate "Maschinen-Maria" which could be controlled externally. And while Homer's version does not suggest a fear of self-sufficient automatons overpowering other characters, later portrayals of mindless automatons often presented them as ominous creatures due to their potential to influence humans, for better or worse. Sometimes driving them to madness, sometimes leading to their creator’s downfall - i.e., as punishment for humanity's curiosity and perceived arrogance to interfere with God's divine creation - these creatures were originally conceived as experiments, specimen without agency, primarily intended to serve and comply. The popular term "robot", however, only emerged in 1921, coined by Czech writer Karel Čapek in his play "R.U.R." (Rossum's Universal Robots). The term derives from the Slavic word “robota”, signifying front-line labor, which is the central theme of the play. The company R.U.R. produces androids that are designed to make work easier for humans. Within the play's narrative, these so-called robots eventually rise to power, leading to the annihilation of the human race. The story dramatically escalates, ultimately leading to two robots who fall in love with each other, metaphorically representing the new Adam and Eve, and thus the beings responsible to repopulate the rest of the planet. R.U.R. is not only interesting because of the first occurrence of the term "robot" or its parallels to the biblical creation of Man. Over the course of the play, it is highlighted that the machines, originally designed to benefit humanity, can also turn against their creators. Asimov’s “I, Robot” from 1950 exemplifies this through its depiction of androids which end up confronting a profound moral dilemma; in order to save humanity from itself, they need to incapacitate all human beings. You may suspect how the story unfolds already... In the same year, when proposed by mathematician Alan Turing, the so-called Turing test became an early benchmark for evaluating whether a machine exhibits human-like intelligence through an assessment of its conversational interaction capabilities (Turing, 1950). Though Turing's own life was tragically cut short, his ideas lived on to build the groundwork for modern AI development. (...to be continued)
